KDA was delisted because the company that issued and supported it announced it was ceasing operations, and venues do not keep markets open for an asset whose issuer has stopped existing. That is the short answer. The long answer is a sequence of dated decisions, most of which stated no reason at all, and it is worth reading in order because the pattern explains more than any single notice.

## You'll see this if

- You held KDA on an exchange and watched it become untradable.
- You want to know whether the delistings were about the technology, the team, or something else.
- You are writing about the network and need the sequence without speculation.
- You are looking for someone to blame and would rather have the timeline.

## What the notices actually said

Almost none of them gave a reason. A typical delisting announcement contains a date, a pair list, a trading cutoff, a withdrawal window and a reminder to cancel orders. Some add a sentence about periodic review of listed assets. Very few say why a specific asset failed that review.

So the honest answer has two layers: the decisions are documented, and the reasoning is mostly not. What follows separates the two rather than filling the gap with a story.

Note the shape. The company announcement came first. Trading halts followed within a week to a month, and withdrawal deadlines trailed by weeks or months. That is the standard wind-down order: stop new exposure, let holders exit, then close the door.

## The cause, as far as it can be established

Three things are documented and a fourth is inference. They are worth keeping apart.

**Documented: the issuer stopped.** On 21 October 2025 the company stated that continuing operations was not possible, and that active maintenance of the chain would stop. That is a fact with a date and a primary source.

**Documented: venues delisted in the following weeks.** The table above is the record, taken from the venues' own announcements.

**Documented: the chain did not stop.** Blocks continued, a community edition took over governance keys in November 2025, and accounts were untouched. No hack, no chain halt, no failed upgrade appears in the record.

**Inference: listing economics stopped working.** Exchanges list assets for trading revenue, and an asset with a departed issuer loses market-making support, news flow and the compliance paperwork that a listed token requires. Notices rarely say this, because saying it invites arguments. But the timing follows the announcement closely, which is what a support-driven decision looks like.

## What was not the cause

Four explanations circulate, and none is supported by the record.

- **A technical failure.** No incident, halt or exploit is documented around the delistings. The network kept producing blocks.
- **A regulatory action against Kadena.** No enforcement action appears in any of the notices. Delistings were commercial decisions by venues, announced in the ordinary way.
- **A token migration or swap.** None happened. The token is unchanged, which is why old addresses still work.
- **A community or governance collapse.** Governance moved to a multisig in November 2025 without interrupting block production.

The distinction matters for holders deciding what to do next. A technology failure would suggest the asset is impaired. A commercial wind-down suggests the market got smaller while the ledger stayed intact.

## Why venues move in this order

The sequence is not arbitrary. It reflects how a venue limits its own exposure.

1. **Deposits close first,** so no new liability arrives.
2. **Trading stops next,** which fixes prices and prevents a rush into an empty book.
3. **Withdrawals stay open longest,** because closing them while balances remain is the outcome venues most want to avoid.
4. **The deadline passes,** and unclaimed balances become a support process rather than a market.

Understanding that order is useful in any future delisting, not just this one: the moment deposits close, the clock on your options has started even though trading still looks normal.

## What it meant for holders

- **Anyone who acted inside the window could exit.** The windows were announced, and they were short.
- **Anyone who missed it kept the asset.** The balance did not vanish; the route to a buyer did.
- **Anyone holding on a venue past a deadline** moved from a market problem to a support problem.
- **Anyone self-custodying** was unaffected by every row in that table. The delistings were a venue event, not a ledger event.

That last line is the practical lesson, and it is why the record matters more than any warning.

## FAQ

**Did KDA get delisted because the project was a scam?**
No. No notice alleges fraud, and the delistings followed a public announcement that the company was winding down. Venues reacted to an issuer exit, not to an allegation.

**Was the delisting coordinated?**
The dates cluster tightly, which suggests venues responded to the same event rather than to each other. There is no evidence of coordination, and no need for it to explain the pattern.

**Is KDA still tradable anywhere?**
Some venues still show a market, and availability changes. Check the venue's own deposit and withdrawal pages rather than a price page.

**Does a delisting affect the chain?**
No. A venue removing a market changes who will buy, not what the ledger records.

**Why did some venues keep withdrawals open longer?**
Because the alternative is holding customer balances indefinitely, which creates a bigger problem than a delayed exit. The deadlines differ by venue policy, not by asset.
